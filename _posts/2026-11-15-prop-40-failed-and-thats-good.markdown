To the chagrin of many of my progressive peers, Prop 40, California’s proposed retroactive one-time wealth tax on billionaires, failed at the ballot box, with nearly <a href="https://calmatters.org/politics/elections/2026/11/california-election-result-propositions/">53% of voters</a> rejecting the proposal.

I sympathize with the concerns about the ballot prop’s failure; amidst <a href="https://www.ppic.org/publication/income-inequality-in-california/">widening income inequality</a> between high- and low-income earners[^1] and <a href="https://calmatters.org/commentary/2026/06/newsom-legislators-budget-reality-deficit/">reckless deficit spending</a> that could threaten <a href="https://calbudgetcenter.org/app/uploads/110728_Where_Do_State_Tax_Dollars_Go_pb.pdf">funding for public services</a>, not to mention the <a href="https://calmatters.org/politics/elections/2026/11/prop-40-billionaire-tax-defeated/#:~:text=Billionaires%20spent%20%24378%20million%20to%20defeat%20Prop.%2040%2C%20exposing%20a%20rift%20among%20California%20Democrats%20on%20taxing%20the%20rich.">inordinate amount of money</a> that was spent by billionaires to kill the measure, it seemed that passing a tax on The Worst People You Know was the right fiscal policy to meet the political moment.

Unfortunately, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heartbreaking:_The_Worst_Person_You_Know_Just_Made_a_Great_Point">The Worst People You Know Made A Great Point</a>. Prop 40 was not only poorly designed, but its uncertain short-term revenue gains came with potentially substantial long-term fiscal costs.

First, the primary purpose of taxation is to raise revenue for public goods and services. Economists have <a href="https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/11/02/g-s1-151009/two-economists-studied-californias-billionaire-tax-they-reached-wildly-different-conclusions">quibbled</a> over the dynamic revenue effects of the proposed wealth tax, and unsurprisingly, come to conclusions that suit their <a href="https://x.com/PhilWMagness/status/2115812484032393689">ideological priors</a>. <a href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w35899">Rauh and Jaros</a> estimate a negative net present value of the tax; while they claim the tax itself would raise around $30B, the <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/07/california-billionaire-tax-collection-challenge-01108639">out-migration of billionaires</a> erodes the future *income* tax base, with the present value of lost income tax collections exceeding the one-time wealth tax revenue by nearly $39 billion. Rauh and Jaros make some fairly aggressive assumptions about billionaire flight, use an <a href="https://x.com/MichaelSLinden/status/2113319267030688208?a=hFeZ6siiykdoenRNFSs&s=20">infinitely long time horizon</a>, and bake in a 50% chance that the wealth tax fails in court and collects nothing, which all tilt their estimates significantly more negative, but on the other side of the debate, <a href="https://youtu.be/KLTDkpxKHLs?si=c_YEHj46qEcW3rLK&t=583">Gabriel Zucman’s assumptions</a> of *zero* billionaire flight and failure to account for future lost revenue are overly optimistic at best and border on delusional at worst.

The nonpartisan <a href="https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026">Legislative Analyst’s Office</a> finds that while the tax would raise short-term revenue, though how much is highly unpredictable due to both administrative challenges and volatility of wealth, there could be an ongoing decrease in state *income* tax revenue of <$1B per year as a result of out-migration by the wealthy. Courtesy of Policy Engine, you can actually <a href="https://www.policyengine.org/us/california-wealth-tax">build your own wealth tax</a> and see what would happen to revenue estimates based on different assumptions. In any case, the fiscal outlook is highly, highly uncertain, which, if anything, only builds the case against the tax as a useful or effective revenue generating tool.

In the absence of a revenue estimate I’m willing to stand by, I think there are other reasons to oppose wealth taxes on the fiscal merits. For one, most countries that imposed wealth taxes <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/eu/wealth-tax-impact/">later repealed them</a> or significantly watered them down, largely due to poor revenue generation from long-term slowing of asset returns and erosion to the tax base from capital flight. Additionally, because wealth taxes are levied on a *stock* of held wealth rather than the *flow* of income, you get extremely weird scenarios where you’re taxing the same base twice (e.g., if your wealth comes from wage earnings, the wealth tax is imposed *on top of the income tax*) and run into situations where someone’s <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/eu/wealth-tax-impact/#:~:text=reformed.%5B9%5D-,Wealth%20Taxes%20Generate,and%20Could%20Be%20Confiscatory%20in%20Some%20Instances,-Net%20wealth%20taxes">tax bill is larger than the liquid income</a> they actually have. This is compounded by the fact that the <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/state/california-wealth-tax-billionaires-proposal/">language of Prop 40 uses *voting shares*</a> in its valuation of a company, which are often disconnected from actual ownership in the company.

Take DoorDash founder Tony Xu, for example. He owns just 2.6% of DoorDash, but also has a controlling 57.6% vote share; under an interpretation of the wealth tax law, those super-voting shares could have been treated as non-publicly traded assets subject to taxation based on the 57.6% voting control rather than the 2.6% economic stake. DoorDash had a <a href="https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/dash/market-cap/">market capitalization of around $94B</a> at the end of 2025, implying that Xu would be taxed at 5% of 57.6% of $94B, or a tax liability of $2.6-2.7B despite the fact that his actual ownership share is worth only $2.4B. Additionally, because selling his shares to pay the wealth tax would also trigger a capital gains tax, his total tax bill would come out to over $4B, exceeding 170% of the value of his DoorDash holdings and rendering him bankrupt (theoretically at least, but he would almost certainly qualify for some sort of administrative relief).

More generally, forcing founders to liquidate shares for a tax bill they may not even afford leads to perverse incentives around company control and could force founders to liquidate substantial holdings, potentially depressing share prices and hurting employees and ordinary investors whose compensation or retirement savings depend on these companies. Even with Prop 40 offering the option to defer payments in five annual installments, the outstanding balance would still accrue interest at 7.5% annually, doing little to change the incentives around liquidation, especially when businesses have volatile valuations that are administratively difficult to assess for tax purposes.

<a href="https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/californias-billionaire-tax-act-threatens-growth-and-fails-address-structural-deficits/2026/06/09/7w61g#sec-2-4">And all for what?</a> A one-time tax that won’t raise enough revenue to finance public spending on its own, while doing nothing to meaningfully address deficits and purging the state of long-term income tax revenue?

Another problem with the tax is what it signals about California’s economic environment as a whole. With the exodus of several founders in anticipation of Prop 40, the wealthy do not expect the “one-time wealth tax” to actually be “one-time.” And to be fair, there is precedent for temporary taxes in California becoming permanent; <a href="https://calmatters.org/politics/elections/2026/10/prop-3-california/">Prop 3</a>, appearing on this November’s very same ballot, made a temporary top marginal income tax rate from 2012 permanent after it was already renewed once in 2016! I’m optimistic that voters rejecting Prop 40 reshapes expectations around the state’s stance on wealth taxes and signals a commitment to pro-growth policy, but the possibility of the wealth tax re-appearing on the ballot makes for a highly precarious fiscal environment in the near term.

As I said, don’t feel bad about the wealth tax not passing. It was poorly designed and the projected long term fiscal drag is not worth whatever one-time revenue gains were on the table, especially with the risk of founders moving operations and high-income workers to other states. To whatever extent taxing the wealthy is a moral issue more than it is an economic one, there are better tax systems to accomplish those goals.

But what should California lawmakers do instead if issues of inequality and poor public funding are paramount? One option is to just turn to better taxes that don’t have all the shitty incentives of a wealth tax; <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/blog/taxing-consumption-progressively-tax-the-wealthy/">consumption taxes</a>, for example, avoid issues of double taxation entirely while not incentivizing the “borrow against your own wealth” strategy employed by the ultra rich (they would be taxed on consumption with borrowed funds).[^2]

Another option is to adopt a more pro-growth, and yes, pro-business, tax code overall. The economy is not zero-sum! It is not the case that the ultra wealthy being richer necessarily comes at the expense of low-income earners, especially once you factor in redistribution, and sometimes a rising tide really does lift all boats. There are a bunch of easy pro-growth reforms California could adopt, such as <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/state/state-net-operating-loss-provisions/">ending California's recurring suspensions of net operating loss deductions</a> so companies aren't taxed on future profits without accounting for losses from previous years; modernizing its outdated depreciation system and allowing <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/state-tax-index/states/california/">accelerated first-year expensing</a>; and, if you want a tax that would actually tax evil rich people (homeowners) while also being pro-growth, fucking <a href="https://calmatters.org/commentary/2026/10/proposition-13-california-loophole-taxes/">reform Prop 13</a> so these pampered boomers stop leeching off of artificially deflated property tax rates so local governments don't have to rely on exorbitantly high sales taxes because of an anemic property tax base (thereby creating incentives to actually build new housing so <a href="https://siliconvalleyathome.org/prop-13-is-making-things-more-expensive/">young people can buy homes at more affordable prices</a>). In doing so, we'd create a more hospitable environment for investment and growth and expand the tax base over the long term rather than hemorrhaging <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/state-migration-trends-map-americans-moving-population-changes/">our population</a> (and our electoral power[^3]) due to a high cost of living and an unreasonably large tax burden.

To my progressive friends, it’s fine to dream big; I’m all for standing tall against the sinister schemes of the wealthy and standing up for the public good. But please, dream bigger than shitty tax policy.
