## My Thoughts on the Rent Freeze

A few days ago, New York City's Rent Guidelines Board approved Zohran Mamdani's <a href="https://time.com/article/2026/06/26/new-york-rent-freeze-stabilized-apartments-zohran-mamdani-housing-promise/">rent freeze proposal</a> that he campaigned on in 2025. I have <a href="https://arnavg.github.io/jekyll/update/2025/06/25/you-were-lied-to-about-rcv.html">written about Zohran before</a>, and while I was initially skeptical of how effective he would be as a legislator, I can say now I'm quite impressed and my opinion on him has only improved over time. This is largely because, in my view, Zohran has abandoned his more left-leaning instincts in favor of strategic moderation, including securing meaningful wins on <a href="https://www.politico.com/news/2026/05/26/mamdani-affordable-housing-land-use-00935614">zoning and permitting reform</a> (more <a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/06/04/business/mamdani-housing-new-york-city">here</a>) that expand the supply of housing in NYC, and importantly, Zohran himself has said that he has come to <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2025/06/10/nyregion/zohran-mamdani-interview.html">acknowledge the role of markets</a> in tackling the housing crisis.

Unfortunately, the rent freeze proposal is a backslide into anti-market tendencies that I think are harmful on net.

Rent control as a whole is a policy that is anathema to economists. <a href="https://kentclarkcenter.org/surveys/rent-control/">Surveys</a> of academic economists continually find that they overwhelmingly believe rent control reduces the supply of available rental housing, consistent with what basic supply-and-demand models would predict as a result of a <a href="https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/price-ceiling.asp">price ceiling</a>. However, we need not defer to theoretical toy models to make the case against rent control; indeed, a preponderance of empirical literature suggests that rent control significantly constrains rental housing supply and worsens housing shortages, to the point where the resulting increase in market rents offsets gains to protected tenants. In <a href="https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20181289">San Francisco</a>, for example, an expansion of rent control in 1994 reduced rental supply of small multi-family housing by 15 percent and reduced renters' mobility by 20 percent. Additionally, because existing rental properties were converted into higher-end, owner-occupied condos, the resulting influx of affluent renters to these properties combined with lower mobility for incumbent renters likely worsened inequality via gentrification in the long-run.

What's more, we have ample evidence suggesting that supply-side zoning, permitting, and land-use reforms that make it easier to build new housing – policies which Zohran has himself endorsed and pursued – *are* effective at lowering rents and combatting the housing crisis by alleviating shortages. Cities like <a href="https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2026/03/18/austins-surge-of-new-housing-construction-drove-down-rents">Austin</a> and <a href="https://reason.com/2025/07/22/rent-prices-are-falling-fast-in-americas-most-pro-housing-cities/">Minneapolis</a> have been beneficiaries of such reforms, with steadily lowering rents by pursuing density and easing land-use restrictions rather than turn to price controls as a fix.

To be charitable to rent control proponents, the existence of this literature is not a definitive case "against" passing rent control laws on its own. That's because:
1. There is nothing normative about economic theory or the econometric literature; i.e., we need not enact rent control just because *some* tradeoffs exist and *some* agents may be harmed. While rent control may hint at prospective renters and the housing market at large being negatively impacted, *incumbent* tenants having their rents and rate of displacement lowered might be a legitimate policy goal worth pursuing, and the decision of whether to do so via rent control is not mandated by a descriptive literature. There is a very real political economy to deciding who bears the cost of these tradeoffs, and policymakers opting to protect current tenants is a "valid" political choice in this sense. Is this <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rent-seeking">rent-seeking</a>? Sure, but an <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/neoliberal/comments/15uxis7/me_when_i_talk_to_nimby_homeowners_luddites_and/">economist's favorite insult</a> is not an argument.
2. Downstream of this, some rent control proponents go as far to acknowledge the tradeoffs of rent control, and argue that those in favor of market-based reforms should also support the expansion of public housing (à la <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_Vienna">Vienna</a>) to combat the constraints on housing supply created by rent control.

While these are defensible counterarguments, I believe that they are both myopic and fall flat under real-world scrutiny.

For one, regardless of whether you are okay with prospective NYC residents – college students, young professionals, and international immigrants alike – being unable to move to the city due to supply constraints, it's not clear that limiting the mobility of incumbent residents is beneficial to them. While rent control proponents often tout lowered displacement as a benefit of the policy, preventing tenants from getting priced out of the city, there is <a href="https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282803769206188">empirical evidence</a> suggesting that one consequence of tenants staying "locked in" to their current leases is that families that experience changes in household composition – due to marriage or divorce, or the birth of a child or children moving out, for example – find it extremely difficult to upsize or downsize their rental units to fit their family needs. If you, as a tenant, have ever wanted or needed to move for non-rent-related reasons, the result of longer tenancies in rent-stabilized units pushes both current and hopeful residents into market-rate housing that may only be affordable to affluent renters as landlords raise the price of new market-rate housing stock to compensate for revenue shortfalls. As evidence from San Francisco shows, the result of this is heightened income inequality and gentrification that worsens affordability in the long term.

While it's fun to dunk on landlord greed for Internet clout, the mechanism by which the price of rental units increases is not sufficiently explained by a corporate greed theory-of-everything. As <a href="https://truthonthemarket.com/2025/03/13/prices-are-signals-and-politicians-keep-shooting-the-messenger/">Brian Albrecht writes</a>, "prices are signals, and politicians keep shooting the messenger." The rising cost-of-living for New York residents *includes* rising input costs to building development, and while operating costs still continue to increase while a key revenue lever in rents is frozen, the financial stability of the New York housing stock becomes increasingly precarious. <a href="https://www.stern.nyu.edu/faculty/bio/arpit-gupta">Arpit Gupta</a>, the lone no-vote on the Rent Guidelines Board's approval of the rent freeze, <a href="https://arpitrage.substack.com/p/the-rent-freeze-and-a-return-to-the">writes</a> what a worst-case scenario of this ratchet might look like:

> The city has been through this cycle before. During the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s, large numbers of rent-regulated properties fell into financial distress, which resulted in **foreclosures** and eventual takeover by the city. Further freezes could produce similar results by making **more properties financially unviable**. Turning the ratchet further, without corresponding reductions in costs, threatens to turn a large portion of the rental stock into an expanded version of the New York City Housing Authority: more buildings owned or effectively controlled by government and increasingly dependent on public support. The lesson is that freezing the price of a service indefinitely while its costs continue to rise does not produce cheap or abundant service. Instead, it produces **deteriorating assets** and, eventually, **public bailouts and takeovers**.

Because a rent freeze reduces the incentive for incumbent tenants to leave their below-market-rate units, landlords, in the face of increasing operating costs, have little incentive to make improvements to existing units, both due to financial constraints and a lack of tenant pushback due to constrained mobility. Currently, <a href="https://cityjournal.substack.com/p/the-high-cost-of-new-yorks-rent-freeze">10% of rent-stabilized units</a> operate at a net loss in income, and these units are also disproportionately likely to rack up housing violations as a result of degrading maintenance in the face of higher costs. With the move from a second-generation rent stabilization regime to a more aggressive first-generation rent freeze, we would expect both an increase in units subject to operating losses and a consequent worsening of housing quality, as well as reduced housing supply as developers face lower economic returns to construction, increasing the price of new, market-rate housing. Looking forward, the freeze compounds supply-side damage, as tax abatement programs like 485-x already link property tax relief to affordability and rent stabilization requirements, and extending the freeze to newly constructed units under these makes financing new development infeasible. The net result is a two-tier housing market in which incumbent tenants benefit from below-market rents while prospective renters face exorbitant listing prices for new market-rate units, the very dynamic politicians decry when arguing for rent control in the first place.

To put it more succinctly, if the goal of a rent freeze is genuinely to improve the lives of renters rather than to win elections, benchmarks of policy success should include whether renters have better quality housing, more freedom to move, and a city that remains accessible to future residents, in addition to looking at changes to the rent itself.

Given the enormous economic costs of rent control, in the form of rental prices for the housing market at large as well as increased mobility frictions, degrading rental unit quality, and reduced development incentives for future housing stock, the gains to tenants in the form of reduced and predictable rents seem only marginal in comparison; and importantly, to the extent that predictability and security of tenure are genuine policy outcomes worth pursuing, (1) the second-generation rent stabilization regime that was already in place provided exactly that, without the more severe market distortions of a full freeze, and (2) this goal would be more effectively accomplished by expansions of the housing supply that keep rent-levels low across the market, allowing for both resident mobility and lower rents in the face of ample supply.

To put it more bluntly, it's not worth <a href="https://www.econlib.org/rent-control-is-worse-than-bombing/">nuking the housing market</a> to the political benefit of entrenched, rent-seeking incumbents.

We ought to reject the framing that people who want to move to New York City have a less legitimate claim to affordable housing than current residents. Today's outsider is tomorrow's tenant, and focusing the political economy of rent control *solely* on the incumbents who benefit dampens long-term growth and the health of the housing market at large. The popularity of a policy that concentrates benefits on organized incumbents at diffuse cost to everyone else is not, on its own, a sufficient justification for said policy; liberals and progressives already selectively accept this logic when it comes to their opposition to tariffs protecting domestic industries or immigration restrictions protecting incumbent laborers, neither of which survive scrutiny just because their beneficiaries show up to lobby for them. If you care about the national housing crisis, change starts at the local level, even if you, personally, may not financially gain from it.

Lastly, I wanted to touch on the advocacy for social/affordable housing espoused by rent control proponents. While it is true that flooding the housing market with new, affordable units would decrease rents, there are immense <a href="https://housinginpractice.substack.com/p/how-much-does-it-cost-to-build-subsidized">financial and political barriers</a> to building both government-owned public housing as well as privately-owned affordable housing. The costs to build and maintain even private affordable housing are sky-high and <a href="https://apps.urban.org/features/cost-of-affordable-housing/">borderline infeasible</a> without significant supply-side reforms to zoning and land use. Furthermore, pursuing this exact same expansion of the housing supply via <a href="https://www.bisnow.com/national/news/affordable-housing/affordable-housing-is-an-oxymoron-why-homes-for-low-income-renters-are-far-more-expensive-to-build-130527?utm_source=outbound_pub_5&utm_campaign=outbound_issue_88081&utm_content=outbound_link_11&utm_medium=email">privately-developed market-rate housing</a> is significantly cheaper and already has a proven track record of feasibility and economic gains to renters at the local level. In California, for example, burdensome permitting and land use regulations make developing affordable 1.5 times more costly than market-rate alternatives.

It's also not clear that rent control is effective as a temporary solution en route to a more comprehensive public housing regime. NYC's own history suggests that aggressive rent regulation in the face of rising costs does not produce a stable holding pattern while better alternatives are built out, but rather a deteriorating housing stock that becomes a political and financial liability of its own. Even setting aside whether the stopgap works, there is no principled basis for treating incumbent tenants as the natural beneficiaries of interim relief; prospective residents waiting decades for social housing to materialize have an equally legitimate claim to affordable housing.

As much as it can be argued that YIMBY-style market-oriented housing reforms also face political barriers, (1) we have myriad examples of these reforms passed at the municipal level already, and (2) we are making bi-partisan progress at the national level, most recently with the <a href="https://bipartisanpolicy.org/issue-brief/inside-the-deal-whats-in-the-final-21st-century-road-to-housing-act/">ROAD Act</a>. Combined, these reforms offer both political and financial feasibility that public housing just cannot compete with in the near-term. The fact that a rent freeze has become the policy tool of choice to combat the housing crisis creates asymmetric political durability where rent control is easy to pass but nearly impossible to reverse, since the incumbent tenants who benefit from them are precisely the organized, politically-active constituency that shows up to vote and lobby. Supply-side reforms, by contrast, require sustained political capital to enact and are already facing headwinds despite modest recent legislative wins. Layering a rent freeze on top of nascent supply-side gains undermines the very mechanism by which those reforms lower rents, making new housing development less financially viable and potentially calcifying the new first-generation rent freeze regime into a permanent feature of the housing landscape. As the history of rent regulation in New York makes clear, "<a href="https://www.therebuild.pub/p/the-newest-argument-for-price-controls">temporary price controls need not stay temporary</a>."

The purpose of this piece is not to bash rent control proponents just because I have a policy disagreement. I think we all have a common goal of more affordable housing, but my preferred mechanism of accomplishing this goal looks a lot more like Zohran's previous zoning deregulation than his current rent freeze, which I feel undermines his policy wins on housing from last month. To the extent that this proposal's downstream negative consequences can be mitigated, Arpit Gupta <a href="https://www.vitalcitynyc.org/mamdani-rent-freeze-agenda-nyc/">suggests targeting the most distressed buildings directly</a> by (1) conditioning rent guideline increases on building maintenance records, so landlords who let properties deteriorate forfeit increases until violations are remedied; and (2) pairing upzonings of distressed stabilized stock with pathways for market-rate development that can cross-subsidize struggling regulated units. I think housing market doomsday scenarios as a result of the rent freeze are unrealistic, but it's worth keeping in mind the economic tradeoffs to such a policy and what we give up by forgoing a more committed supply-side approach that ensures a fair housing market for all.
